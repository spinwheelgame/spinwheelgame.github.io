Where the Randomness Comes From

Every spin starts with a random number, generated the same way most everyday software randomness works: a pseudorandom number generator produces a value that's unpredictable enough for real-world use, even though it's technically calculated rather than physically random like a coin flip.

From Number to Landing Slice

That random number is mapped onto the wheel's total rotation — for example, a value between 0 and 360 degrees. Add several full rotations for visual effect, apply a slowing animation curve so the wheel decelerates naturally, and the slice under the pointer when it stops is your result.

Why Every Slice Has an Equal Chance

Because the random number is drawn from a flat, uniform range and each slice occupies an equal share of the circle by default, every option has the same mathematical probability of being selected — a wheel with 10 options gives each one exactly a 10% chance, regardless of order or position.

Quick tipAdding duplicate entries (like listing "Pizza" twice) intentionally weights the odds — a simple trick if you want certain outcomes to be more likely.

Why It Feels More Trustworthy Than a Coin Flip

A visible spin with a slowing pointer gives everyone watching the same information at the same time — there's no hidden step to question. That transparency is part of why spin the wheel tools are popular for group decisions: the process is exactly as visible as the result.
